Studying the Scripture as a Community

By Will Walker

4,357 words · 19 min read · Article

Part of the series Postcards from Corinth

In brief

A chapter excerpt from Will Walker's book on community as the context for spiritual growth, focused on studying Scripture together rather than in isolation. It argues that privatized Bible study undermines real engagement with the text, using personal stories and blog dialogue to push back on treating the Bible as a mechanical 'operating manual' rather than the story of God's redemptive work.

Bible study & interpretationCommunity & fellowship

Kingdom of Couches • Chapter Excerpt

Community is not simply another ministry activity; it is the context for all ministry activities. It is the environment in which spiritual growth takes place. And since community is central to our model for spiritual growth, a few planned socials are not going to get the job done. We needed a resource to elevate community, encourage it, and teach it.

Will Walker’s book is probably the best apologetic written for why community is the matrix for spiritual growth. But the content goes well beyond theological reasoning, providing extremely practical how’s and why’s for living out our faith in community. Through excellent writing and reasoning, personal examples and stories, the book creates a hunger and desire for communal living. Content of Book: Why We Pursue Independence; Why We Need Community; Repentance; Community and the Word-Communal Quiet Times, Community and Confession; Group Prayer; Godly Speech and Conversation, and A Commitment to Live Communally.

Communal quiet times

When my friend John was in college he was invited by a high school ministry to interview for a leadership position in their ministry. John is a sharp guy and had no doubt shown some potential. The meeting went well and it looked as if John was ready to take the next step. Then the interviewer asked him, almost in passing, “By the way, what version of the Bible do you read?” John replied, “I think it’s the Martin Luther King James Bible.” “Huh,” the man said, “John, what do you say we do one more semester of training.” The means by which we learn how to follow Jesus are varied: circumstances, prayer, contemplation, conversations, and so on. But central to our understanding of relationship with Christ and missional community is what we learn from the Bible. This seems obvious, but for some reason we are prone to get bored with the obvious. And usually boredom leads to neglect.

I asked a dormroom full of guys to share how they think they are doing spiritually. The responses varied, but for the most part they felt like they were not doing very well. They talked about how they go days and weeks without feeling God’s presence, without even thinking to pray, just doing life in their own strength. They said they sort of wake up every now and then and realize how long it’s been since they felt like they were walking with God. I asked them to explain what causes them to go into these lulls of flying on autopilot. They mentioned things like busyness and selfishness, but nobody contended to really know how things get that way. Just for fun I asked them how many of them had been reading their Bibles. The first guy to respond said about two to three times a week. “It’s two to three,” he said, “because sometimes I don’t show up to our Monday discipleship group. The other two times are this group and our large group meetings on Tuesday.” I rephrased: “How much have you read the Bible lately on your own initiative?” Across the board the answers were in the range of not very much to not at all. Then I stated the plain truth that was slapping us all in the face: “You can’t expect to escape autopilot if you are not immersed in what God has to say about life.”

The written revelation of God must be at home in all methods of learning. Community is important in that light because we all interact uniquely with the Scriptures. This is the nature of our blog community, to write about how the truth of Scripture inter-acts with the day-to-day realities of our lives. We learn from each other by partaking of the Bible together.

Most Christians agree that the Bible is important, but in general I think our privatized approach to learning what it says under-mines our respect for it. In other words, what we learn on our own does not match up with how much we really do value the Bible. It’s been ten years since I started reading and studying the Bible. A wonderful lady taught me the method of observing, interpreting, and applying. She taught me about determining the author’s intent in light of the historical and cultural context, and how to cross-reference verses and look up words in the original language. I’m sure I didn’t even know there was an original language. I was well on my way to understanding the Bible all on my own. It was great. That is until I discovered that I had understood knowledge all wrong. I treated it like something static, contained in some region of my brain where I could reference it if it were to come up in conversation. This is not the kind of knowledge spoken of in the Bible. Jesus and Paul, among others, talk about knowledge as something much more experiential. + + 8 0 I am not opposed to Bible study methods. They are critical to accurate interpretation. I just think that if we rely solely on our own ability to execute step-by-step methods, we risk approaching the Bible too mechanically, too subjectively.

Bob was reading a commentary one time and came across this quote from the author: “God’s operating manual for the human machine that He created, is the Bible.” I am not going to pin all of our problems on this statement, but I have heard people refer to the Bible this way: operating manual, instruction book, survival guide, and so on. Fortunately, Bob will not let you get away with a statement like that. He posted this response on the blog that day, probably that minute:

Isn’t that reassuring? If you run into any problems with the “human machine,” just consult the “operating manual” and fix the problem. It’s that simple. Cause and effect. Problem and solution. But God didn’t create a “human machine.” He created humans: the crown jewels of His creation, the ones who bear His own image. If we were machines, life would be a lot easier. The fact that we’re not is what makes life confusing and complex. (Sometimes I wish my wife were simply a ‘human machine.’ Then I could consult the ‘operating manual’ and figure her out.) Apparently the “biblical scholar” (phrase used loosely) who wrote the article has never pondered the books of Ecclesiastes, Job, or Psalms. It’s hard to see exactly how those books fit in the “operating manual.” Reading his quote made me remember how much I hate simplistic, formula-driven Christianity. The Bible is not God’s “operating manual for the human machine.” It is the story of God’s interaction with humankind. It is a complex, layered, intense portrait of God’s goodness and our brokenness. It is the cosmic drama of redemption that is being worked out in history, with the end goal of God being glorified in all the earth, gathering worshipers for Himself from every tribe and tongue and nation. Heaven will not be a warehouse of human machines, but a community of redeemed human beings. Father, forgive the simple scholars, for they know not what they obscure.

I told you, Bob’s a preacher. We love that about him. The funny thing is, I suspect Bob secretly aspires to be a scholar himself. Nobody reads operating manuals for pleasure, because they tend to be boring. Brett gets the impression that some of the students we meet with find the Bible boring. They only read it because they are supposed to or because they need to find a solution of some sort. Very few read the Bible because they long to discover the depth of mystery that is God and man and relationship between the two. One day at Peet’s Coffee, Brett and I were talking about the Gutenberg press and how our methods of Bible study were hardly possible for the first fifteen centuries of the church. Old and New Testament Christians did not read the Bible. They heard it from those who knew the stories or had the letters. This doesn’t mean that we shouldn’t read it, of course, but perhaps there is some-thing to simply reading it aloud instead of jumping head first into “study.” Maybe this is why, even after his articulate letters, Paul still expressed desires like, “I long to see you, so that I may impart to you some spiritual gift to strengthen you, that is, that we may be mutually comforted by one another’s faith, both yours and mine.”1 1 Romans 1:11-12, NIV + + 8 2 This is a post Brett wrote about his experimentation in reading the Bible aloud with students:

The other night I watched the movie Primer with several friends. Primer is not an easy movie to under-stand; in fact, I left quite unsettled. It all came at me a bit too fast, without the proper narrator asides or flash-backs to let me know what was really going on. Just tension. The kind of tension that leads the nocturnal nerd in me to read posts on message boards from equally curious people with far more time on their hands. As I began to read plot analysis and scene by scene breakdowns, I started to feel a sense of loss. In attempting to understand every detail, I found myself understanding the movie’s point less and less. Dissecting a frog can be helpful in figuring out its inner workings, but it also tends to kill it.

I sometimes wonder if our attempts at dissecting the Bible are the same way—bringing a sense of knowledge while removing all of the life. You’ve heard it said that God’s Word is living and active, sharper than any two-edged sword.2 This sounds mysterious, exciting, a bit unsettling. It makes me want to read the Bible a lot more than I do. Of course, it also makes me want to look up a verse on peace or understanding in the index at the back so I can diffuse those emotions and move on with my day.

When I look at the Bible, I see a series of books and letters important enough to be copied for years by hand, letter by letter. Words pregnant with mystery and understanding, life and death. Stories, poetry, prophecy, heartfelt asides between apostles and churches. 2 Hebrews 4:12 Quite meaningful really. Then I look at the veritable textbook of a Bible I have sitting on my shelf: full of crossreferences, clear interpretations of confusing passages, and a concordance for looking up every mention of the word “recompense.” I think this reflects the importance of the Bible, I’m just not sure it retains the meaning of it. Great, valuable thought has gone into understanding this great, valuable book; it’s just that this wealth of information makes my own need to know seem much less valuable. Dissecting a poem will help you understand it, but handing all of these interpretations to someone else will not help them love it. Over the past few weeks, a friend and I have been getting together to read Luke’s Gospel. We’ll head over toSpider House, grab some coffee and a table, and start reading. Out loud. He reads for a bit, and then I’ll read for a bit. We’ll stop every now and then to talk about what we find fascinating or what we don’t under-stand or what we’ve never noticed before. Often we’ll be reminded of events taking place in our own lives. Occasionally we’ll stop and pray.

Reading Luke’s words about Jesus and His message, I am refreshed and challenged, even though I’m some-times left a bit confused. (It’s coming at me a little fast, without the proper flashbacks or plot analysis.) I feel this urge to get out a commentary, but I avoid it. The tension I feel pushes me to keep reading, to understand more. I find myself not only reading things that are true, but I also find this truth to be full of emotion and meaning. It feels very much worth believing. I am amazed at the faith of Zacharias and of Mary and of the Roman centurion, surprised at what their trust in God says about my own. I love seeing Jesus’ clear sense of mission coupled with His demonstrative love of individual people. + + 8 4 I suppose we’re reading the Bible as literature, nothing extraordinary. Though in reading its words in context, and without Cliff Notes, it’s impossible to escape how much more than literature—or a textbook on life—the Bible really is. Maybe the reason people have a hard time picking up the Bible is because someone else has already picked it apart.

My intention is not to diminish the value of methods or to vilify scholarship. My sincere hope is to stir something inside you that moves you to read the Bible afresh, to implore others to join you, to pursue truth and meaning with the aim of finding life in the person of Jesus.

So where does the Bible fit into spiritual life? For most people it fits into what they have come to call “quiet times.” I was taught to have quiet times, first as a child in the corner, and then as an adult at the coffee shop. Both are wonderful and should be treasured as part of the American way of life. What I don’t understand, though, is how the Bible got mixed up with quiet times the way it did. Never have two simple words so shaped the way people think about what it means to study the Bible, or to be a Christian for that matter. The approach has become common, even prescriptive:

  1. Find a quiet place, maybe some music in the back-ground, coffee/tea, comfy chair, etc.
  2. Read something in the Bible. Read for at least fifteen minutes (this seems to be the minimum requirement).
  3. If you have questions get them answered immediately by reading a commentary (warning: reading multiple commentaries will only lead to more questions).
  4. Journal and/or pray.
  5. Impress someone with what you have learned (optional, and for experienced Christians only).

This would by most standards be considered a good approach to Bible study. That it may be, but it is not the best approach to learning. I am biased, of course, because I fail miserably at the whole quiet time agenda. It’s been awhile since I have read the Bible this way: privately, quietly, devotionally. I have read and discussed it with people, read it to prepare for talks and small groups, written about some of its passages and ideas, and read a few complimentary books along the way. So either I am not a good Christian or it is possible to learn the Bible and love Jesus/people apart from this approach. Perhaps both are true.

It seems to me that the Bible is meant to be read aloud, heard in community, talked about at dinner, applied to actual life, debated—anything but confined to the comfy recesses of my “devotional life.” I am not suggesting that we should not read the Bible alone. My friend Jonathan points out the dangers of throwing out personal reading: “Personal choice and discipline are critical for cultivating intimacy with the Lord whenever one is apart from the community. If this discipline is not developed, the community will become a crutch and not a catapult in the aim for holy living.” If you benefit from personal Bible study, don’t neglect it. I am just saying that if devotional reading is your only approach to learning the Bible, you will likely be disappointed with the results.

I like to read. I like to talk about how I like to read more than I like to read, but nevertheless I manage to read some books now and then. Last year I read Renovation of the Heart by Dallas Wil-lard. I enjoyed it so much that I recommended it to all my friends. Seeing my enthusiasm, they asked, “What’s it about?” This is the point when I realized that I couldn’t articulate much once I got past the very obvious. It was terrifically meaningful when I read it, but somehow it lost its place in my mind and life. This happens + + 8 6 a lot. I’m always assuming that I understand what I read, and it isn’t until I have to talk about it that I see how much I still don’t understand. I expect the same goes for stuff we have read in quiet times past.

Just the other day I was leading a discussion and trying to write down what someone had said. I knew what she was saying, but when I tried to capture it in writing, I got stuck. I just wrote down, “the thing that Melinda said,” and told the group I would know what that meant when I typed up the notes. Seizing the opportunity to make me live by my own advice, Jeremy said, “If you can’t say it, you probably don’t know it.” So we kept talking about the idea until we could articulate it; that is, until I could see that I really didn’t understand what I thought I knew. All I am saying is that people see things differently, and when we talk about what we know, we allow others to show us a new perspective, and vice versa. Solomon said it this way: “He who states his case first seems right, until the other comes and examines him.”3 Paul wrote to the Colossians about learning and understand-ing spiritual truth. He said, “Let the word of Christ dwell in you richly.”4 That verse, read alone, would seem to promote devotional reading. But the context indicates that both the means by which we let the word dwell and the purpose for letting the word dwell are overtly relational and communal:

Therefore, as God’s chosen people, holy and dearly loved, clothe yourselves with compassion, kindness, humility, gentleness and patience. Bear with each other and forgive whatever grievances you may have against one another. Forgive as the lord forgave you. And over all these virtues put on love, which binds them all together in perfect unity. Let the peace of Christ rule in your hearts, since as members of one body you 3 Proverbs 18:17, NIV; 4 Colossians 3:16, NIV were called to peace. And be thankful. Let the word of Christ dwell in you richly as you teach and admonish one another with all wisdom, and as you sing psalms, hymns and spiritual songs with gratitude in your hearts to God.5 This sounds more like group therapy or even a wild party than a quiet corner in the coffee shop. If the only place the Bible gets to “dwell” in your life is your quiet time—not in your conversations and communities—I think you might be surprised at how much assumed understanding you have and, quite frankly, how boring the Bible has become to you.

You may still be asking why you can’t learn in private. I didn’t really say that. Knowledge is multi faceted, and what I am saying is that the highest priority knowledge—experiential knowledge—is what is inseparable from community. You can have quiet times that are neat and tidy with, “one thing you could do today to apply what you learned.” This is a legitimate means of learning. I don’t do this very much anymore because there is too much assumed understanding and not enough frustration or opportunity to be wrong. It’s too packaged and pleasant to affect actual life. Some of us argue that we need time to get our thoughts together. That’s fine, but I wonder if what we really mean is that we need time to get our act together. Honest contemplation (getting our thoughts together) depends on our ability to distinguish between God’s voice and our own thoughts. That is why we need to invite other people into the process, because we can’t see our own face. We need people to ask us questions, impose their views and experiences on our understanding of things, challenge our complacency, encourage our growth, force us to articulate our thoughts, enable us to see ourselves in ways that we could never see on our own.

5 Colossians 3:12-16, NIV + + 8 8 In reality, learning is probably an organic process. We contemplate, we process with others, and we contemplate again, always refining. What has been gutted from the process is the communal hearing, learning, and processing of the Word. We practice Christianity the same way we go to school—we study on our own and come in and take a test. It’s a lousy model for Christian growth and, incidentally, a pretty lousy academic model as well. Our spiritual climate is dominated by a privatized mindset with regard to learning and growing: self-help, devotionals, quiet times, and the phenomenon that I can go to church and not have to talk to anyone. To turn the whole quiettime approach on its head, I propose something quite revolutionary. I offer to you the TV devotional, wherein you get up every morning to snuggle up on the couch and spend an hour or so watching your favorite shows (TIVO makes this all possible). Then, during the rest of the day when you would normally watch and talk about TV with other people, you could read and talk about the Bible, especially as it interacts with your actual life. I call this the communal quiet time. An evening might be spent with family or friends reading and talking about Scripture, contemplating and cross-examining the ideas, allowing biblical truth to dwell in your community. I am not opposed to being alone or taking time to read, reflect, or pray. I just think that the Bible speaks to us generally noisier than our quiet times will permit.

Communal quiet times are not complicated. You just need a Bible and at least one other person. Getting started is the hardest part, which is true about every kind of discipline.

When Debbie and I started doing this together, we decided that we would not have any agenda for discussion. We just took turns reading aloud 1 John. We talked about a few things we noticed as we went, but mostly we just enjoyed sharing in the reading of Scripture together. We haven’t read anything since then, not because we didn’t benefit from it, but because habits are hard to form. Sometimes you have to get started several times. Again, you will get frustrated if you want relationships to get better. Reading aloud together is merely a simple exercise that will help you jump-start meaningful conversations.

Brett and I work on a lot of small group materials, so we discuss passages with a very specific agenda of getting to the core issues and figuring out how to help others get there too. Usually we read a passage and start talking about the stuff that jumps out at us, things we find interesting, suspect to be important, or have questions about. We often find that we read the same verses in very different ways. That is why multiple perspectives are so important to learning. Sometimes we try to articulate what the passage communicates in one simple sentence. This forces us to read for under-standing. You would learn a lot just by trying that with people. The ideas in Scripture that have had the most profound impact on me are from passages that Brett and I have discussed in this way, including the ideas in Philippians that I wrote about in chapter 1. Brett and I found communal quiet times to be so helpful that we wanted to try it out with a group of students. We asked them to pick a number between one and twenty-seven (there are twenty-seven books in the New Testament). Once they picked the number, we turned to that book and asked them to pick a number between one and however many chapters were in that book. We figured between the two of us we would be familiar enough with the “randomly” selected passage to wing a good discussion. But the lot was cast to 2 John. Neither of us had ever read 2 John. I’m not sure I even knew there was a 2 John.

We began by reading the passage aloud, and then we asked people to share their first impressions—Not interpretations, just stuff they noticed. After that we had everyone write down five questions that, if they could have answered, would help them understand what the passage talked about. They wanted to know, + + 9 0 of course, who the “chosen lady and her children” were and if the “dear lady” was the same person, not to mention the “chosen sister.” There were also questions about the antichrist reference and what it meant that they could lose what they had worked for. The weirdness of this passage gives you an idea of how we were able to spend an hour talking about people’s questions and trying as a group to find answers. I was shocked by how insightful the discussion was and by how much I learned. I left feeling very sober about the need to endure in the work of loving people. We had similar discussions with other groups about various passages and/or topics, all of which were personally and communally stimulating. Once you have the framework in place, I suppose there are dozens of ways to have a communal quiet time. Again, the elements are simple: people, a Bible, and a desire to learn in such a way that the ideas become meaningful in actual life. Execution, however, is not always easy. Our conversational habits and personal insecurities sometimes make simple communication and learning more complex. In spite of all the talking we do—maybe because of all the talking we do—we tend to forget the value and power of words. We need to think more intentionally about the role of conversation in personal growth and in the life of community.

Most people do not think of conversation as a spiritual discipline, but we think conversation might be the most practical and fruitful discipline at our disposal. That’s why we devoted the next chapter to it.
