## If God Isn’t There

Well let’s see, if God isn’t there, we are forced to agree with the insane notion that creativity, art, speech, love, morality, justice, compassion and courage evolved by happenstance. Hmm, what are the odds; perhaps tomorrow I’ll find an iMac sprouting out of my neck, leaving my PC head to swing in the trees of evolutionary obsolescence. While possible, the belief in such ingenious happenstance might take more faith then I’ve got.

So I’m going to assume that God is there and that evil is there and that somehow we can make the two jigsaw pieces fit without ripping the cardboard all to hell. Perhaps a stroke of genius or ignorance on my part—you can be the judge of that—but it has always seemed to me that the Scriptures (Judeo/Christian- Old testament/New Testament) provide an insightful perspective on the problem of evil, as opposed to other religious books where evil came to us on the back of a sea turtle or something to that effect.

And then there is the spiritual component of man. Wherever you go in the world people are innately religious. Atheism must be learned, while theism is reflexive, as is a belief in an afterlife. But no one has ever proved there’s an afterlife. What would you suggest: someone die and then come back and tell us what it was like? It’s called a Near Death Experience and they happen all the time. There is considerable uniformity to the experiences, and people do in fact continue to exist though their vital signs have flat-lined. I’m not sure what more evidence could be provided here.

## The Good Book

The Bible does not hide the reality of human suffering or evil, actually highlighting some of the grocer varieties from our human history: rape, incest, human sacrifice, torture, it’s all in there, and described in a manner worthy of an NC-17 rating. The story of Genesis, and Adam and Eve in particular, was meant to communicate some fundamental truths about the human condition: why it is, as it is, and not as it should or could have been.

And then there are all the existential issues that race through our curvy grey roads of cerebrum: assumptions that life should have purpose, that people should be loved, that we were made for something better, that rights are inalienable. From wherest come thy existential hard drive?

And what of the moral side of man? Don’t we sense that torturing children is always wrong and not just a cultural belief—well sure, if you live in Greenland I guess torturing kids is alright. If our morality is simply derived from our society who or what is a moral The story encapsulates these general principles: Man was a special creation endowed with the thumbprint of a Creator, which was manifest among other ways in our free will; God created something good, man and woman, and something equally good, free will. By its nature, free will makes for two unimaginable possibilities: a person can freely choose to love you, as opposed to being programmed to do so, and they could choose to reject, even hate, even murder you. (What comprises God’s knowledge is difficult to say, but I believe He not only knows what will happen in the future, but He knows all possible futures: possible worlds he could have created including worlds where free will did not exist. I therefore assume that out of all possible worlds, a world containing freewill was more desirable. And, out of all of possible worlds where man was given freewill, this is the best possible world: the sum total of all contingencies yielding the greatest good however God would define “greatest good.”)

Returning to the Garden (and if it’s any reflection of the downward spiral of evil, I believe that geographically theologians place the actual location of the Garden somewhere near Falluja, Iraq), mankind sinned and evil entered in to the Creation. Genesis clearly states that evil was not a part of God’s original design, but a consequence of human sin (the perverse use of free-will). With that choice of rebellion, the relationship between man and God was severed, and like links in a chain it ruptured every relationship contingent to it: man with man (wars, murder), man with woman (divorce, broken families), man with nature (alienation), man with himself (guilt, shame, fear). The negative experience of these severed relationships is what we refer to as suffering. Like a broken DNA sequence it has produced all manner of deformity and suffering.

As I think we’ve all noticed, the world that we live in—the world that God actualized—might not be optimal for happiness, but it may be optimal for “soul-making:” the creation of great souls. I’m not sure, for instance, where courage, compassion, mercy, or justice might be created except in the crucible of a bent world. These are latent human capacities that can either be developed or not, and as we make choices with our will, they become actual virtues. And until we do act on them they are nothing more then hypothetical capacities, sort of like the wings on a turkey.

A second possible reason for allowing our current world to have lapsed into a Mr. Smith infected Matrix is ironically found in the final chapter of Genesis. After witnessing the outbreak and epidemic of the virus of human evil in chapters 1 to 49, Genesis, chapter 50, hands us this analysis.

You intended to harm me, but God intended it for good to accomplish what is now being done, the saving of many lives. (Genesis 50:20)

The physics of evil seems to corroborate the biblical sequence of events, as there is no original evil. Try as we might, evil is always a parasite of good. You cannot have bulimia without something good to pervert, like eating. You cannot have destruction without something of value to destroy. Evil is always a bent good. In other words good must have been prior to evil.

God, the author of creative activity, demonstrates that the ultimate act of creation is not making something from nothing, but making gold out of manure. The ability to bring good out of evil is roughly the definition of redemption. And it would seem that as courage can only be seen in a broken world, so too redemption. The creative activity of redemption, the experience of being redeemed, and the virtue of being a redeemer, are a part of why I believe God has allowed evil. As I write this, the Super Bowl has just ended and the coach is fighting back tears as he holds up his humongous trophy. The victory, the battle, the struggle, the overcoming, the joy, the relief, the peace: perhaps God feels this makes for a better story and existence then the living coma of nirvana—I do. Something of the physics of our own experience corroborates it as well: the greatest portion of evil and suffering in the world is the result of human choices: As of today I count 26 wars going on in the world, and on a personal level I have envied, lied, hated, lusted and spent $13.50 on lunch and Starbucks that could have saved a child from starving, and that’s just since 9:00 o’clock this morning.

## What Could Possibley Be Worth It?

If, as I have suggested, God knew the outcome of giving us freewill, he must have seen a greater good in allowing it, but what? Again, the Scriptures, though far from exhaustive, provide clues.

C.S. Lewis once said that “God whispers to us in our pleasures, speaks in our conscience, but shouts in our pains: it is His megaphone to rouse a deaf world.” The scriptures allude that another reason God has allowed this mosh-pit of a world is that we might “cry out” and “return” to God. Happy and content people don’t see their need for God, desperate people do. Trauma and calamity shake the very foundations of our life and force us to reexamine ourselves: our beliefs, lifestyles, and choices.

And while we’re discussing this ideal world for soul making it might be instructive to make an observation about the physical laws of such a world. It would need to operate on strict cause and effect. Think for instance, if God were to intervene every time someone shot a gun, turning the bullets into marshmallows (yum). It is only in consequences and the predictability of consequences that we are able to act as true moral agents. I mean if God’s is always going to keep my car from crashing, why even look where I’m driving. A soul-making world is one of strict cause and effect, and therefore, by design, one in which divine intervention wars against its very purpose. ## A Word to the Left Brain

While necessary, that was admittedly academic and heartless, and yet the problem of evil is emotional as well as intellectual. It’s not simply “why do people suffer?’ but “Why did my mother suffer?” As a Christian, the following passage, to me, is one of the most powerful in the New Testament:

As he approached the town gate, a dead person was being carried out—the only son of his mother, and she was a widow. And a large crowd from the town was with her. When the Lord saw her, his heart went out to her and he said, “Don’t cry.” (Luke 7:12,13) I find it of enormous comfort to read that Jesus’ “heart went out to her.” Intellectually it helps me to know that God has reasons for my suffering, but I am comforted that God is pained by my pain: that he is not distant, uncaring, or unmoved by my suffering. The scriptures are saturated with such expressions of God’s compassion, the Mosaic Law going as far as to legislate against animal suffering. I do not know what pain you’ve experienced or what event or events would lead you to read this article, but the message of the Scriptures is that God deeply cares and is moved by your suffering.

It also states that there will be a day, when God will put an end to evil and suffering. Our present time in the crucible will seem as nothing:

He will wipe every tear from their eyes. There will be no more death or mourning or crying or pain, for the old order of things has passed away.” (Revelation 21:4)
