## Rick James

There are two types of people in the world. (A good way to begin an article as you’re compelled to see what kind of asinine over-generalization or false dichotomy could possibly follow.) There are those who believe that inside every person is a soul or spiritual dimension, and there are those who believe that the only thing deep inside us is our intestines. Consider the phenomenon of “the enduring sense of self.” Your cells are ceaselessly dying and replicating. You no longer possess the same cells you had at birth, yet you have a cohesive sense that you are still you. Therefore, “You” are not simply your cells. Remember when Millhouse bought Bart’s soul for $5—boy, that was a scary episode. Bart’s soul, if you remember, was a translucent Bart. Philosophically, all that is meant by a soul is a non-material or spiritual aspect of who we are: something that cannot be seen under a microscope or lava lamp and most likely survives our physical deaths. Unlike Bart’s soul, it is doubtful that a disembodied soul has any appearance whatsoever, for any appearance, even a translucent one, constitutes a body. But as the cartooning of Bart is outsourced overseas to cell animators getting paid a chicken’s egg for a day’s wages we cannot be overly critical. And with that social criticism we will now turn to the evidence for a soul.

Or what about the objectivity of the self: you distinguish yourself from your experiences and emotions. When you squash a fly, there’s no reason to conclude that its experience is separate from itself. In other words, in its tiny fly brain, it senses, “I am pain” (if it does feel pain). You, on the other hand, distinctly feel that pain is happening to you and that you are distinct from the experience of pain. The same could be said of emotions: when I’m enraged, I feel that a mood has overtaken me, but I am distinct from the mood; I might even try to fight off the mood or act as though it is not effecting me. And then there is PMS… (What do you think I’m nuts? I’m already offending half the populace arguing for a soul.) The point being, “You” cannot be distilled down to simply feelings or emotions.

## Inadequacy of a Purely Physical View

The person who does not believe in a non-material (read spiritual) component of man must explain every human function biologically, and that’s a difficult assignment, as noted by MIT-trained scientist Gerald Schroeder, “Consciousness has all the trappings of a nonreducible element of our universe. The conscious mind is not mystical, but it may be metaphysical— meaning out of the physical.”

Or when you are thinking about a pink elephant, where in your brain is there an actual image of a pink elephant? If we were to tear you apart piece by piece, at what point would we be able to find a clump of cells and put them under a microscope and locate the QuickTime file of the Pink Elephant?

Furthermore, there is the “aboutness” of the mind; it is always daydreaming, planning, remembering, envisioning, out for a night on the town and usually returning way past curfew. It’s as if our mind would wander away if it weren’t tethered to our brain. Of the many attributes of consciousness not easily reducible to natural or biological processes we’ll highlight just a few:

Wilder Penfield, the renowned neurosurgeon, did a series of experiments on epileptic patients. Using electricity, he would stimulate certain regions within the brain, causing the patient to move his or her arm or leg or head. The patient always knew that he or she was not the one moving the body parts. In other words, the patient clearly had a sense of existence apart from the interactions between the brain and body. Probing the entire cerebral cortex, Penfield concluded that there was no place that could be electrically stimulated to cause a person to believe or decide. and immediately sat down and drew up plans for the Pyramids.

For years people have tried to reduce humans to nothing more than a series of drives (Freud’s life and death drives, for example) and instincts. But Cambridge professor C. S. Lewis proposed the following thought experiment:

In the book The Case for a Creator, philosopher J. P. Moreland makes the following observation: “I know that consciousness isn’t a physical phenomenon because there are things that are true of my consciousness that aren’t true of anything physical.” He goes on to give the following example:

Let’s say you heard a woman screaming. For a moment you would sense two different instincts, says Lewis. The first is self-preservation: get out of there as fast as you can. The second is a herding instinct— woman in trouble, must save (the herding instinct is primitive and therefore doesn’t use pronouns). But what do you choose to do, asks Lewis?

Some of my thoughts have the attribute of being true. Tragically, some of my thoughts have the attribute of being false. However, none of my brain states are true or false. No scientist can look at the state of my brain and say, “Oh, that particular brain state is true and that one is false.” So there is something true of my conscious states that are not true of my brain states, and consequently they can’t be the same thing.

Your consciousness chooses between the instincts, and it is as different and separate from them as the pianist is from the keys he chooses to play on the piano. The consciousness sits over and above our instincts, drives, and desires, and it chooses which it will act upon. Therefore consciousness cannot be reduced to either an instinct, drive or desire. So difficult is the problem posed by our consciousness that Laurence C. Wood stated that, “Many brain scientists have been compelled to postulate the existence of an immaterial mind, even though they might not embrace a belief in life after death.” It is in the face of such evidence that perhaps the worlds most renown philosopher and atheist, Oxford professor Antony Flew, recently changed his position to the shock of the philosophical world, concluding that some sort of intelligence or First Cause must have created the universe. “A super-intelligence is the only good explanation for the origin of life and the complexity of nature.” (Though the succinctness of the quote makes it seem like he was a hostage being forced to read it)

## Did Our Souls Evolve?

Strictly speaking, I am making a case for the existence of a soul, and not where it came from. But the inability for evolutionary theory to account for the existence of the soul or consciousness is far from irrelevant. The ability to speak distinguishes man from all apes and hominids. Most hominids had small, apelike brains and no capacity for language. Then, suddenly in the fossil record, man appears with several unique features, including an enlarged brain capacity with no clear-cut links between the two.

## Near Death Experiences

In 2001, Dutch cardiologist Dr. Pim van Lommel, working in the Division of Cardiology at the Hospital Rijnstate in the Netherlands, published the results of a ten-year study, “Near-death experience in survivors of cardiac arrest” (I’m sure the movie title will be more compelling). In the report, Near Death experiences were defined as “the reported memory of all impressions during a special state of consciousness, including specific elements such as out-of-body experience, pleasant feelings and seeing a tunnel, a light, a being of light, deceased relatives, or a life review.”

In other words, patients who were clinically dead, experienced consciousness, but not simply that: they experienced very similar phenomenon: seeing a light, leaving their body, a life-review, etc.

In an interview with Dr Pim van Lommel, he was asked, “What could be doing the perceiving since it is not a conscious brain, eye and ear system? This was his answer: to this category the billions of stories that touch on paranormal experiences, from premonitions concerning the future to religious visions. And while 99.9% of it is probably dribble, the sheer quantity and universality of the experiences make it an argument not easily dismissed. These phenomena all point beyond our physical bodies to a mind or soul, and perhaps point even further to an Intelligent Designer, a First Cause, God.

Exactly, exactly. That means when there is no activity in the brain, let’s say a flat EEG, and people can still have their full consciousness with cognition, emotion, with memories from their youth, all for the possibility of perceiving. That means [the soul] is something outside your body that you can receive in your body by your brain. The continuance of consciousness in the absence of recorded brain wave activity provides powerful evidence for the existence of a soul.

## Last Thoughts

If the answer to the question of who we are is “a unique hybrid of body and soul” it really does have profound implications. One of which may be the answer to the second question, “Where have we come from?” The existence of a soul clearly implicates a Creator, as evolution speaks to biology not metaphysics, by which I mean, it is an absurdity to conceive of evolving a soul (non-matter).

## Reflexive Morality and Spirituality

The spiritual and moral impulse of man is perhaps the most epistemologically intuitive of all the indicators of a soul. We experience that impulse in some of the following phenomena:

Religion: People are innately religious, with over 90% of the world’s population believing in God. Wherever you go, people instinctively pray and bow toward heaven. It would seem that belief in God is not something people are reasoned into, but something they must be persuaded out of.

Oughtness: Most humans have a sense that things are not as they should be. We struggle with circumstances, resent death, complain of evil, and have a general sense that we were made for something better: that things “ought” to be different. Why do we have these thoughts? Why shouldn’t we simply accept life on its own terms?

If the soul is a Creator’s thumbprint then the very nature of our soul may also provide some clues to the nature of God. We might, for example, extrapolate that God is a moral, creative, loving, spiritual, aesthetic, relational being. But that’s another topic. My closing point is simply this: that all of the experiences described seem to be pointers to the existence of a soul, and a soul seems to be a pointer to the existence of God, which in turn points to the stupidity of Bart for selling his soul. And with that, I’ll stop pointing. Morality: The Nazi’s appealed to culturally relative morality at the Nuremburg Trials, but no one was buying it because we intuit that no matter what the culture, there are certain actions that are intrinsically right and wrong. There are, most assuredly, cases where right and wrong are culturally defined, but it does not negate the intuitive objectivity that torturing children and the sex slave trade are wrong no matter who is practicing them.
