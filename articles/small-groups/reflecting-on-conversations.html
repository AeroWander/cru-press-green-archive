Reflecting on Conversations

7,400 words · 32 min read · Article

Part of the series Postcards from Corinth

In brief

A chapter excerpt from Will Walker's community book examining why people talk the way they do, opening with the author's own tendency to use conversation to seek approval rather than connection. It contrasts this 'thirst' for validation with Jesus as Living Water, then hands off to co-author Brett Westervelt's reflections on the deeper motives behind everyday conversation.

Kingdom of Couches • Chapter Excerpt

Community is not simply another ministry activity; it is the context for all ministry activities. It is the environment in which spiritual growth takes place. And since community is central to our model for spiritual growth, a few planned socials are not going to get the job done. We needed a resource to elevate community, encourage it, and teach it.

Will Walker’s book is probably the best apologetic written for why community is the matrix for spiritual growth. But the content goes well beyond theological reasoning, providing extremely practical how’s and why’s for living out our faith in community. Through excellent writing and reasoning, personal examples and stories, the book creates a hunger and desire for communal living. Content of Book: Why We Pursue Independence; Why We Need Community; Repentance; Community and the Word-Communal Quiet Times, Community and Confession; Group Prayer; Godly Speech and Conversation, and A Commitment to Live Communally.

Conversation

Sometimes I wake up with what feels like a hangover. As best as I can remember, an actual hangover feels like bankruptcy—spent, impoverished, regretful. That is how I feel some mornings, only without the obvious explanation for why. I still have to get up on these days, if not for work then because my four-year-old is jump-ing on the bed. So I’ll drag myself across the floor to the bathroom. My friend Rick says that you are never more yourself than when you are on the toilet. “It is the place of ultimate humility,” he says. “Can you think of a place where you would least want to be seen than on the toilet?” I tell you this because the last time I felt bankrupt, God spoke to me precisely in that place, the place of humility. He kept bringing a verse to my mind: “Everyone who drinks this water will be thirsty again, but whoever drinks the water I give him will never thirst.”1 Jesus told that to a woman one day when she had gone to draw water from a well. He was saying that the water from the well would only last for a little while. Eventually she would have to come back and draw more. He was using water, something she depended upon for survival, to reveal Himself as the Living Water––what she needed for life.

1 John 4:13, NIV + + I have plenty of water, that’s not the problem. I can turn on the faucet and watch it trickle out for hours. I don’t because that would be irresponsible, but I could. No, the thirsts in my life are for things like acceptance and fun and deeper meaning. The wells I go to are as real as the thirsts––working or watching television or talking, for example. Talking. Conversation is such an easy place for me to go to find what I’m looking for. I use my words as bait for things like approval and value––temporary redemption. I say things, often not because I mean them, but because I want to sound cool or smart or funny. I want to convince people that I’m the kind of person that means the things I say.

This usually works. For a time. The reality is, though, that when you try to get what you need from the wrong places, you just feel empty again. When you keep drinking from these wells, you only find temporary satisfaction. Conversation is important. It’s central to our lives. But it’s often draining and empty. I talk so much about things that don’t matter, about how traffic’s bad during rush hour or about how it’s taking a long time for our waiter to bring us our food. People know these things; they don’t need to be made aware. Even worse, sometimes my words are directly hurtful. I like to blame most of these instances on other people’s ignorance or inability.

I feel bad about sticking my foot in my mouth; I never intend to. I wonder though, if I would slip up so frequently if I approached conversations out of an interest and love for people instead of a desire to get people to like me? That sounds simplistically noble, and it is. But our words quickly come to reflect what we’re really hoping for, regardless of how hard we try to mask it. I need water, but I’m in even greater need of Living Water. When we get what we need from the right source, instead of from a temporary well, we can rest because we are internally satisfied. Jesus says that this internal kind of satisfaction is like a natural spring—fresh water, always flowing. We would always have something to give because of this. Giving would come naturally because we would not need + + 9 4 to take from anyone, not need to be redeemed by what others can give.

The reason you feel so terrible when you wake up after drinking too much is because your body is dehydrated. The dryness inside gives you an awful headache. What you drank has actually taken out more than it has put in. Conversations are full of these shallow wells, promising life but actually making life more miserable. The more shallowly I drink the more I dry up, dehydrated, hung over. Silence is no more an answer than refusing water, our words just need to be valued for what they are worth––which is sometimes more and other times less than we think.

I have spent a lot of time in conversation with Brett over the past year or so. While I am always talking, he is always observing: Why do people say the things they say? Why are people saying so many words and yet communicating so little? How do I lovingly get Will to stop talking for a few minutes? I guess you could say that Brett has walked me through more than a few hangovers. After wrestling with this chapter—and losing—it finally occurred to me that Brett has probably thought more about the dynamics of conversation than anyone I know. So I asked him to write this chapter. He probably wanted to write it all along but couldn’t get me to stop talking long enough to tell me. That’s okay. I like when things are my idea anyway. Without further babble from me, welcome to the thoughts of Brett Westervelt.

This is a chapter about conversation. About why we say the things we say. In all honesty, I don’t know exactly what motivates the words we speak or withhold, and my own motivations are often murky. The words we read and hear each day come from a thousand different angles for a thousand different reasons. It’s the same with our conversations, where language has its back and forth; where it’s often what goes unsaid that speaks the most. I + + won’t pretend to have the guidelines for each and every one of the verbal interactions that ground our lives; that would be foolish of me, and would take a long time. Instead I’ll offer some looks into my thoughts on the matter and bring in some second opinions–– different angles on the same thing––hoping that we find some-thing insightful here. These thoughts may not line up perfectly; they might overlap or exist completely independent of each other. But getting a group of people together to discuss something usually leads to that. There’s a certain beauty in the disconnectedness. Conversation is frustrating to me. I tend to be pretty optimistic about life in general, sometimes wondering why the glass has to be only half full. I also tend to (over)analyze life, constantly develop-ing theories and philosophies that I’m sure must be right because they make perfect sense to me. Optimism and theorizing can be problematic as they’re quick avenues away from reality. Conversation is frustrating to me precisely because it’s so utterly grounded in what is in fact the case.

It’s quite easy for me to start thinking that a girl likes me. I can think those thoughts and interpret situations that make her undeclared love for me the only realistic possibility. Maybe I ran into her at a coffee shop where she smiled at me in a way not unlike the way Meg Ryan smiled at Tom Hanks in that movie You’ve Got Seattle. It’s fate. It’s the ongoing conversation we have in which she finds ways to casually mention her boyfriend that clue me in to the reality of the situation.

I always picture my friends and I, having these amazing discussions that sometimes lead to punches being thrown and other times lead to tears. I like to think we’re all pretty passionate. I’ll watch a movie like The Royal Tenenbaums or The Breakfast Club and think it likely that I will soon find myself exchanging witty dialogue with lots of hidden meaning that eavesdroppers will carefully analyze. This rarely happens. It turns out none of us have anyone to write our lines.

I hear people talk about the lost art of conversation, how con- + + 9 6 versation used to be an art form and how it’s much more like coloring within clearly marked lines now. Like in a coloring book. I imagine this to be true, though I have no such past art to compare my current conversations with. One thought is that we don’t know how to talk to other people because we watch television too much and our brains have atrophied. I wonder, though, if the real reason our conversations are often so draining and empty is because we’re afraid of each other.

We are afraid of what people might think if they knew what we actually thought. We fear looking stupid. I’m fine if the conversation stays on impersonal topics like sports or the weather or how that guy’s outfit does little to compliment his body type. I tend to speak my mind about those kinds of things, because there’s not a whole lot hinging on my opinion. Wait, you think it’s unseasonably warm this fall? That’s ridiculous. Our friendship is over. It’s when things get personal that I begin to keep my actual thoughts private. Ask me what I think about gay marriage or if Hindus go to hell or how I feel about the outfit that you are wearing, and I quickly become guarded. I’m much more likely to spout out the right answer or the easy answer instead of my actual thoughts, coloring well within the lines of social protocol. It’s not that I really want to be dishonest. The pretense is draining; it’s dehydrating. It’s just that honesty is risky; it’s rarely politically correct. It’s easier to lie or to just keep things impersonal. What I sacrifice along the way is meaningful relationship, keeping people at arms length for fear of losing them. I could term all lighthearted conversation “shallow,” labeling small talk as the enemy here, but it’s not. I’m willing to debate at great lengths the tracks essential to a Ryan Adams “Best of,” and frankly, Vince Young’s Heisman chances are an important topic of conversation. If I were female, I might even find it in my heart to spend a few hours discussing the latest issue of InStyle. Talk of this sort is a lot of fun, and it’s the normal person’s way to transition to more serious matters. It would be strange for me to go up to a complete + + stranger and ask them about the greatest tragedy or triumph they’ve ever experienced.

It’s my attempts at acceptance––to please or appease those around me––that send my conversations awry. I find myself launching into diatribes about things I care little about, like how things written in italics should really be spoken differently. I camp out on the lighthearted and meaningless, even as I am really wondering about entirely different things. I use my words to manipulate, twist, or avoid difficult realities.

A few months ago I was on the phone with my sister, and after we had talked for a while, she could tell there was something wrong with me. I claimed to be tired, and she let it go. Really, I was feeling pretty depressed at the time, and after we said our goodbyes, I went and wrote about that very thing in my journal. It’s not difficult to be honest and bold in a journal. But in conversation, with all of the instant feedback, with fears of being misunderstood, with the risk of being let down, it’s easier to lie. It’s easier to disconnect my thoughts from my words, to color within the lines of expectations. It’s this thinking and speaking disconnect that makes conversation grueling, that drives me to my journal and away from lasting friendship.

There are solutions to this conundrum. Like drinking alcohol. My friend Doug was at a wedding a few months ago when a friend, with the aid of a few glasses of wine, told him that in her opinion, procreation was the only real point to life. Doug, initially startled by the bluntness of this statement, went on to have a good, substantial conversation with this person, someone who had previously been closed off to in any way discussing her beliefs. Our friend Jeremy calls alcohol “liquid courage,” because after you’ve had a few glasses, you find yourself saying what you would have been much too composed to otherwise mention. Being inebriated makes it hard to have both an internal and external monologue, so they usually just turn into the same thing. Meaningful conversation often results, based on real people’s actual thoughts; + + 9 8 it’s just a shame that really knowing someone requires temporary brain damage and a headache in the morning. (One might dare to propose that there is even a divine design behind drinking wine in moderation. But due to the fear of others, exposing myself, risking rejection, causing friction, and being politically incorrect: I’ll keep that thought to myself. Well, at least hidden in parentheses.) Nevertheless, the blog has been a healthier way for me to open up. The thoughts I was writing in my journal became thoughts that I was sharing with others. The writing process has allowed me to let go of these thoughts slowly instead of all at once. I can read what I’ve written to make sure it speaks to what I’m trying to say before passing it on to Will or Bob or David––a trusted few that I’ve gotten past trying to impress. Finally, I’ll post my thoughts on the blog for anyone to read and comment on. I’ve found myself writing things I never thought I would even admit to pondering, and as a result I’ve found myself in engaging, thought-provoking conversation. It’s not what I would have expected. Along the way I’ve developed some deep friendships––I’ve found people worth crying with or punching. People that I genuinely know and love. Will and I spend the bulk of our days with an extraordinary group of students at the University of Texas. One of the girls I am getting to know is Tina. Tina is refreshing to be around because she’s honest but not proud. Hilarious but also serious. One day we got to talking about how important conversations are to the health of a community.

Lately, all Tina really wants is to be known, good and bad, and still be liked. She told me that and said that she has been telling a lot of her friends the same thing, hoping they would take the bait. “Nobody’s taking it,” she said. “It’s a good thing I guess—I mean I am relieved for them in a way––because it’s asking a lot.” The irony is that so many people feel the same way and want the + + kind of honest and meaningful conversation that makes them feel known, but do not act on it. Everyone is pretending not to want what everyone wants.

Tina thinks we pretend because we are trying to avoid loneliness. “We start developing personality traits that people attribute to us. If you are known for your sarcasm you play it up. If people think of you as the ‘cheery one’, you don’t let people know that you have bad days. By focusing on a few personality traits, we attempt to create an image we think others will like. If this image is accepted, we can avoid loneliness, at least on the surface.” So I asked her how she pretends. I love what she wrote in response: I hide behind sarcasm. Many people like to say they are sarcastic by nature. I used to feel the same way until last week when I hurt a good friend with some misplaced sarcasm. To make matters worse, I realized I had never let this friend know how much he means to me until after I had put my foot in my mouth. I’m cautious about letting people know just how much they mean to me.

I’m afraid of giving of myself and finding out the feeling isn’t mutual. I know that there are several people in my life that I love that probably have no idea, but there are plenty of people I love that have been the subject of a “well-placed” sarcastic comment. I’m finding that I use sarcasm as some kind of leverage I can hold over other people because I am good at it, and not everyone is. This pretending thing gets out of hand, though, and we not only hurt ourselves but others, because we end up dealing with each other superficially, never giving each other the chance to be known.

Conversation is a world of ironies: I use conversations to make people like or admire me, but the person that people come to like and admire is a phantom, a persona that I’ve carefully constructed to hide behind. It’s the difference between Simon and Garfunkel’s Bridge Over Troubled Water and Kelly Clarkson’s Since You’ve Been Gone. Everyone likes Since You’ve Been Gone––it’s shiny and produced and easy to sing-along to, but there’s little honesty there, no depth of reality. Bridge Over Troubled Water may not be as catchy on first listen, but it will move your soul, often in different ways at different times. Chances are that in a few years you will have forgotten about Since You’ve Been Gone, as great of pop as it may be. It’s just that pop changes, while depth and honesty endure. Phantom people suck the life out of community because they suck out all of the reality and replace it with an easygoing facade. These kinds of friendships are easier on the front end, but they rarely endure.

We all need what we must give. Our stories are meant to be shared; they’re realities that provide both discouragement and encouragement––a way to tell of the past and whether it’s worth repeating. A real self, with a unique personality and a perspective gives life in a way that a caricature never could. And while it’s true that we’re all imperfect, makeup is only a temporary fix, an avoidance of reality. We will only experience conversations to be life giving if our aim is to give life to others. God has set it up this way. Whoever wants to keep life will lose it, and whoever gives life will find it.

Talking with people quickly reveals both our need for others and our deep-seated need for what only God can provide. If we could eavesdrop on our own conversations, we would realize how much of what we say is to get attention or acceptance––to derive popularity and redemption from our friends. And while people are the wrong source for it, redemption is a most proper want, but it will only come from God.

We need people to both know us and love us, but the grace + + they need to deal with our faults will also only come from God. They will be able to forgive, but only because, in Christ, God has forgiven them much. The gospel provides a fertile ground for community, and for the kinds of conversations that community tends to be grounded in. It’s tragic that this is often the opposite of our experience.

Christian community can be the place where we pretend most. David works at a church as a small groups pastor. Small groups are designed for conversation, but if you have ever been in a small group you know how meaningful conversations can be hard to come by. Usually they end up being a contest to see who knows the most stuff. David says the problem is not that we don’t say meaningful things, but we know a bunch of stuff we may not actu-ally believe. He elaborated on the blog:

Last week in a small group discussion a newcomer spoke up saying, “I didn’t know we were going to be this blunt. There are a few other things I probably need to share.” I can’t remember the last time I was part of a small group in which a visitor was struck by the level of honesty and authenticity. All the small group Bible studies, all the Sunday school classes, and all the church services, and none of them have impressed most people with their authenticity. Those gatherings could variously be described as educational, informative, challenging, fun, boring, unfriendly, and convicting—but not blunt. Maybe blunt in teaching, but never in sharing about what’s going on in our lives. Why?

Rather than beat up on the groups for not being more authentic (especially since I’ve led many of them), I want to pursue a different question: Why have I not shared bluntly in those groups when given the opportunity? Why did I ask for prayer for more discipline when the reality was compulsive habits controlled my life and a little more discipline was unlikely to make a difference?

Here’s one example of many that relate to this situation. I’m not sure that I believe Paul when he says there is no condemnation in Christ.2 I know that is true somewhere in the record book of heaven, but I am not so sure about here on Earth, especially in my Bible study. Share about sin or struggle in your life that you have no solution for in that context, and you will wish you had merely passed gas. The response is about the same—awkward shifting and glancing around, a few mumbled comments, a lame attempt at humor to relieve the tension, maybe some advice to avoid this happening again.

But if there really is no condemnation, why should I let other people keep me from being honest? I care too much about what others think about me, and in doing so, I continue to construct a pitifully weak foundation to prop myself upon rather than depending, with all my weight, on God’s truth. I pass on the opportunity to invite others into deeper relationship. Jesus spoke about two men who built on different foundations.

They both heard His words, but only one took action as if those words were true. Somehow I want the firm foundation without the counterculture risk involved in actually living my life there.

2 Romans 8:1, NIV + + This summer I spent a few weeks overseas. There were twelve of us, and we spent our time studying the language and culture of our host country, making friends with students at the university we were at, and praying for and getting opportunities to share the gospel with them.

Culture is a strange thing. You don’t realize how much of your life is only the way it is because of where you live until you live somewhere else for a while. It turns out the rest of the world doesn’t think that English is as important as I’ve come to think. Most of the people where I lived got along just fine speaking a different language entirely. I generally take conversation for granted. This was not the case overseas, because communication that I could actually understand and take part in was infinitely less avail-able. This is what I wrote in my journal a few days after our arrival: I sometimes feel as though words have lost their significance. In a world of information, there are so many words that each word carries little weight. We either say much, with little consequence—in effect adding to the noise or we say nothing at all—choosing to see our lives as boring or our experiences as not worth retell-ing. Both extremes treat words and stories as worthless, not as beautiful and powerful.

It’s entirely different being in a place where I can’t understand the language. The noise around me of idle chatter is not much different than a car horn or a school bell. I find myself starved for communication, whether that be in the Word of God or with friends or on the only English channel on my TV. Words, worthless in their endless supply, have suddenly become valuable again.

My frustration with language brought me to a place of deep dependence on my teammates. I spent most of my days in interest-ing but hackneyed conversation with the locals, attempting talk of basketball or the gospel across a sometimes steep language barrier. It was great and meaningful, but also draining. I would return to my dormitory at night, hungry for pure, unadulterated English. Back at the dorm my teammates and I would spend the rest of the evening crammed in someone’s room, discussing the day and life at hand to a depth that we’d been wanting to go for hours. When we’d all met up in Ft. Worth a few weeks before, there was plenty of silence. We were mostly introverts. In that dorm room though, at the end of a foreign day, we needed conversation, and we got to know each other like we’d known few others before. At the same time we valued our words and tried to avoid using them carelessly. You take amazing care with words when you only get to speak a few.

We spent the brunt of these few hours each night asking questions of each other: What’s your family like? What’s been the hardest thing about being here this summer? What are your top five movies of all time? How did you come to faith in Christ? We would take turns asking questions, often following the answers with “Why?” and always delving deeper. I initially just wanted someone to say something that I could understand, but I quickly found interest in what my teammates were saying, and I wanted to hear more. It’s amazing what you find out about people when you ask them about themselves. In these conversations each of us needed what the others had to give.

Being in a group of Christian English speakers in a primarily non-Christian, non-English speaking country was essential to me. I would have had a difficult, if not impossible, few weeks if not for these others. Back in the States the reality is not as different as my more independent life would reflect. My community is crucial, not optional as I often treat it. I have a great need for the words that only my Christian friends who know me deeply can speak. We + + share experiences and a common language that is unique. Usually, I alternate between noise and silence—forgetting the value of my words to encourage and to challenge my friends, to make them laugh (when appropriate), to console their tears, to keep them aware that God is still near, to remind them that someone understands. If we really have been sent here and are citizens of another country, then a Christian community is foreign to this land. Complete assimilation requires some precious losses, and often we merge with society at large without ever realizing it. Will turned thirty this year, which is an age that is a factor of ten, meriting more celebration than other birthdays. I myself was surprised at his age, because on one hand Will has the wisdom of someone much beyond thirty, and on the other hand he always gets carded at concerts that are 21-and-up. I guess thirty is appropriate as an average between two extremes. Will wrote about this milestone on the blog, and it has to do with what we’ve been talk-ing about—conversation:

I turned thirty this year, which I did not think would be that big of a deal to me. I was wrong. Someone told me in college that your twenties are an adventure: that you should try new things, meet new people, accumulate some experience, and be willing to fail. I have been saying this since then, and also that you don’t need to be good at anything until you are thirty. That is when people start getting old, I thought, and by then you should be able to identify some skills, preferably marketable skills. I am not going for humor here. This has actually been the governing philosophy of my life for the last decade. It is a good one, by the way, and if you are twenty-something, you should believe this. It is very comforting. You should also admit to yourself that you are going to turn thirty in a very specific number of days. This is where I went wrong, and now I am here wondering how you go about figuring out what you are good at. Perhaps I thought there would be some kind of turning thirty Pentecost, where God would magically impart skills. I had no such experience, though I did cry twice this week.

Personally, I never think I am good at anything, but now and then someone tells me that I am. If you look at my life, you will see that I basically spend my time and effort doing things that someone along the way told me I was good at. Maybe I am a people pleaser, but maybe it is also true that what we say to each other in the way of encouragement and confidence is one of the most powerful dynamics in the life of a community. This week people close to me said some things that I did not believe until they said them. Bob told me that he loves me. Brett said he would follow me anywhere, that he would probably do drugs if I did. [Editor’s note: I meant this hypothetically.] Debbie said she believes in me. I can’t tell you how rich these treasures are to me.

I have been reading Philippians lately and cannot help but notice how affectionate Paul is for the people in Philippi. He calls them “dear friends” and “broth-ers” and “partners in the gospel.” He would choose to delay being in heaven to get the chance to visit them again.3 I know that expressing affection for people can be awkward, though I am unsure as to why. Whatever the reason is though, we need to get over it. We need to help people discover themselves, tell them why they 3 Philippians 1:25 + + are a blessing, talk about how God has gifted them and how we see them being effective and fruitful in their faith. We need to tell them that they belong. Everyone is so desperate to belong.

Think about the disciples. Imagine what it would be like to have Jesus (God) look you in the eye and say that you belong to Him, that He believes in you, and that He is going to use your influence to expand His Kingdom throughout the world. In his Gospel, John refers to himself as “the disciple that Jesus loves.”4 I don’t think he was being arrogant. I think this was simply the most central aspect of his identity, that Jesus loved him.

The disciples turned the world upside down. What would the people in your community do if they thought about themselves as the one whom you love? This is the irony of my twenty-something life philosophy: I spent a decade trying to grow and develop so people could tell me how great I am, but it turns out that life is really more about telling others how great God is in their life.

I’m only twenty three, so I have a few years to figure all of these things out, but my limited experience leads me to firmly believe what Will is saying. Encouragement is huge. That’s not overstating things. It’s awkward to tell people the good we see in them. I fear being wrong or overly mushy, but we need to hear those things from each other. The gospel should free us to speak highly of others because we no longer need to solidify our god-status by belittling them. A tendency to discourage bad instead of encouraging good speaks more to an ongoing desire to be important than it does of a love for truth. I have the sense that the Spirit is trying to 4 John 20:2 say a lot of good things through us, but we’re much too busy letting other voices speak—voices of pride, selfishness, and anxiety. There is often no need for the cat to have gotten our tongue; we’ve already misplaced it on our own.

I think Paul alludes to this when he tells the Philippians to do nothing from selfish ambition or vain conceit, but to have humility and have high regard for others. It means having the attitude that Christ had, who didn’t consider equality with God as something to grasp but emptied Himself as a servant.5 Our words are a concrete way to practice humility and to give others regard. Encouragement is serving because it pushes people toward the good that God is renewing in them—emboldening the light, overpowering the darkness. Paul says it more directly in his letter to the Ephesians: “When you talk, do not say harmful things, but say what people need—words that will help others become stronger.”6 I’m not sure it’s possible to distill all we’ve been saying in this chapter, but distillation has its purposes—“Love the Lord your God with all your heart, soul, and mind and love your neighbor as yourself”7 is a God-given distillation of the Law, a clear way to sum it all up. My experience is that most of my empty conversations stem from a self-focused attitude. I think my interactions with people would look entirely different if I approached my words— what and how much I said—with actual interest and concern for others. It’s a heart-level change, really, and “out of the overflow of the heart the mouth speaks.”8 Christ is at work restoring our messed-up heart and the foul words that so often overflow, but we would be amiss to think that we are uninvolved in the process. Our concern and awareness is fundamental to spiritual growth; they result in further dependence on God.

5 Philippians 2:3-8; 6 Ephesians 4:29, The Message; 7 Luke 10:27, NIV; 8 Luke 6:45, NIV + + When I go into a night of conversation with friends having the self-centric attitude of a consumer, I usually leave frustrated and empty. I spend a few hours gathering all I can in the way of being listened to, laughed at, and verbally patted on the back. Get a few people like me together and a competition quickly develops—all of us too busy talking and trying to impress to either learn or listen. No one really hears each other because we are all too busy thinking and talking about our own things.

Of course there are also those nights when it seems like no one has anything to say. These kinds of conversation usually start off with talk of the weather and head quickly and steadily towards silence. Awkward silence. This is a different kind of consuming conversation—I don’t feel like talking, and so I just wait for some-one else to say something. I need conversation (I think that’s part of not being lonely), but I’m going to wait around and let it come to me. When an entire group of people starts thinking this way, complaining or watching television quickly follows the silence. Both are easy and inactive; both sacrifice community. Good conversations involve both consuming and contributing: giving and taking, and taking primarily to be able to give. I listen and then I speak. I listen so that I can respond rightly, not so that I can impress. I won’t ever find the redemption that I’m looking for in the words or laughter of my friends, but I will find needed encouragement. Strong conversation weaves people together, and it only happens as we speak and listen, and as we are more concerned with getting to know others than with proving what we know. Knowledge puffs us up, often beyond recognition. Love edifies.9 9 1 Corinthians 8:1 Conversation is about relationship, and the most foundational element of a relationship is not respect or common interests or even love. It’s honesty. We can know each other only to the degree that we are honest with each other. At the very point that what I think and what I say I think differ, you can only relate to a fake version of me, even if the fake me is just the real me, slightly twisted—true relationship is suddenly not an option. In all of the things we encounter in consumer-driven conversation—shallow topics, saying stupid things, trying to impress, withdrawing—the common ingredient is dishonesty. I realize this is a complex subject and merely proposing honesty seems a bit elementary. I’m not saying that honesty is the cure-all, but I am saying that it will go a along way toward life-giving conversation. Honesty gets us into more meaningful conversations where we feel known and there-fore more interested in knowing others. Our honesty is a contribution that is uniquely our own. It sounds simple, but working it out in actual life is anything but easy. As Bob wrote:

Honesty is an elusive concept. Most of the time what I think is honesty is not honesty at all. My mind is always forming judgments about people. Always. In a room full of people, I’m forming mental categories: that’s the flirty girl; there’s the geeky guy; over there is the kid who talks too much. My judgments about people are often a way of securing my place on the social totem pole. Recognizing my own sinfulness in this area, I usually conclude that honesty is not necessarily the best policy. But now I am changing my mind about that.

A major part of personal maturity is being able to deal with our negative feelings in a godly way. Most of the time what keeps me from being honest with + + people is that I don’t want them to experience negative feelings. It’s easier not to tell the truth and keep the peace than to tell the truth and create tension. By not being honest I manipulate how others get to feel. Protecting them from negative feelings deprives them of the opportunity to deal with those feelings, which keeps them from further sanctification. If people really got their identity from Jesus—his crazy and unrealistic grace and love—maybe I wouldn’t be so hesitant to be honest with them. My honesty, no matter how hard or sinful or ugly, could never undo the honesty of God pouring His love into them at the core of their being. I think that’s the kind of community Jesus intended us to have; I’m just not sure we’re ready to believe the gospel radically enough and do the hard work it takes to create it.

The tension I feel in response to what Bob said is that it’s not always appropriate or helpful to voice everything that we think, and that in our honesty, we could be honestly wrong in our assessments of others. Spewing our honest thoughts whenever we have them is selfishness to the highest degree. I find it helpful to distinguish between selfish honesty and selfless honesty, honesty for the purpose of consuming or for the purpose of contributing. Selfish honesty is characterized by a sense of needing to say something so you can feel better about yourself: gossip, insult, boasting, even confession can be selfish when it’s just about getting it off your chest. Selfless honesty, on the other hand, is characterized by a sense of wanting to say something that benefits others: encourage-ment, admonishment, teaching, personal disclosure for the sake of intimacy, confession for the sake of restoration.

Our habits and inability to know all the factors in a given situation make it difficult to discern our motives. That’s why it’s a process of trial and error. That said, most of us tend to err on the side of dishonesty. Pursuing or even thinking about the kind of honesty that Bob describes is a push in the right direction. In my estimation the most important aspect of honest conversation is facing the truth about our own lives, inviting others into that process, and then humbly disclosing what we discover. We talk as a means of being known as we truly are, not to impress, as we want people to think we are. And as our words do turn to other people, we need to think more about honesty in terms of encouraging one another and telling people the truth about how we see God in them. This is woefully lacking in our communities. I sense that if we told people the truth in the positive sense, telling them the truth in the negative sense would not be as big of a deal. Knowing what to say, how to say it, and when to say it requires discernment, and discernment is not a formula but a process of trial and error. Deciding to be honest is not a one-time purge but a way of life that is navigated through the waters of success and failure over time. Bob was right to say that gospel-centered community is hard work. And perhaps the hardest work is that of transforming our conversations. They have become such a stage for self-importance, and we have so many habits from perform-ing all these years. But we must start somewhere: we need to tell people about the real us, and take interest in the real them as well. As a result of these discussions, our community has developed some talking points. These are things for us to remember as we approach conversation with each other. They are reminders of our reality as believers and as individuals in a community. You might find them helpful groundwork for your own conversations:

  1. God is God: We do not need to solve everyone’s problems, nor do we need everyone else to solve our problems. We do not determine people’s worth, nor do + + they determine ours. We do not know everything there is to know. Not even close. We are not God, but God is, and we approach what we say with this in mind.
  2. You are you: You are not what you say. You can be wrong or stupid and still be valuable, even right and smart in other ways. Do not calculate everything you are going to say to appear better than you are, as if your worth depended on it. You simply don’t know what you don’t know. There is grace to be found in the revealing of our shortcomings.
  3. Talking is talking: If you can’t say it, you probably don’t know it. And you can’t know exactly what you are going to say before you say it. Fearing people’s reactions to what you are going to say hinders honest conversation. You need people’s feedback to your honest thoughts. Only then can you trade your thoughts for someone else’s. It’s like poker. You have to put your cards on the table to get new ones.
  4. Questions are informative: If you want to know something about someone, ask them. If you want some-one to do something, ask them. A direct question gives someone a direct opportunity to answer. Honest questions rightly replace dishonest manipulation. What results is an environment of trust in which you are free to think and pray together about the question at hand, as two people who want God’s will above their own.

Furthermore, questions – even bad ones – usually lead to better questions, and to better conversation.
