## Kingdom of Couches • Chapter Excerpt

Community is not simply another ministry activity; it is the context for all ministry activities. It is the environment in which spiritual growth takes place. And since community is central to our model for spiritual growth, a few planned socials are not going to get the job done. We needed a resource to elevate community, encourage it, and teach it.

Will Walker’s book is probably the best apologetic written for why community is the matrix for spiritual growth. But the content goes well beyond theological reasoning, providing extremely practical how’s and why’s for living out our faith in community. Through excellent writing and reasoning, personal examples and stories, the book creates a hunger and desire for communal living. Content of Book: Why We Pursue Independence; Why We Need Community; Repentance; Community and the Word-Communal Quiet Times, Community and Confession; Group Prayer; Godly Speech and Conversation, and A Commitment to Live Communally.

## it takes a village

We have been telling ourselves a lie. It’s the lie of self-sufficiency. We like to say that growing up means we can make it on our own. The truth of the matter is that sometimes we can’t make it on our own, and even if we can, it doesn’t mean we should. Living in a sizable city like Austin, it’s fairly easy to live an entire day devoid of any kind of human interaction. Actually, it probably requires more effort to be involved with other people than it does to not be. Head to any coffee shop and pull out a laptop, a book, or an iPod, and you’re effectively isolated--free to be in your own world. This kind of independence is extraordinary, really. Machines and technology allow me to be more self-reliant than at any previous point in history. I still need people of course, seeing as I have no desire to live in the woods and subsist on nuts and berries. It’s just that I really only need people from a distance. I need them to be cogs in the machine of my city, to play their role in the economy so I can still get a paycheck.

Few people consciously think this way. We like our friends, we love our families, and we’re secretly grateful for the hungry guy who stands on the street corner with something witty written on a piece of cardboard--he makes us laugh. We have neighbors on the other side of our six-foot privacy fences, whom we talk to on occasion and silently observe the rest of the time, as though we were anthropologists or something. The truth is though, that while we enjoy people, we rarely claim to need them. We put on the air of self-sufficiency as a trump card against pain or inconvenience. It’s okay to like people. It’s just seen as weak to rely on them. The problem with this, as I alluded to earlier when I said we’ve been telling ourselves a lie, is that this is a lie. If you spend some time in a foreign country, Russia for example, where people don’t speak English, you’ll witness an interesting phenomenon: it’s nearly impossible to show up in Russia and just start speaking with people and reading things. You’ll find yourself quickly being shown up by three-year-olds (and probably laughed at). You’ll be quite helpless, really, until you run into someone who can speak English or until you learn the word mojna, which in combination with the proper gesture means “that,” “may I,” or “I’m from America and I wish I could speak the language, but I can’t, so can you please stop laughing and help me?” The key difference, in this case, between a grown man such as myself and a three-year-old Russian boy is that he’s been in a community of people speaking Russian for his entire existence, and I’m still trying to wrap my mind around the idea that a three-year-old is effectively smarter than me. Communication is just one of the many things we only can learn by being immersed in a community. Community doesn’t just provide the skill set for our existence; it also provides the context. Author Dan Allender puts it this way: “You can’t see your own face.” When I read this, I wondered how it was that Dan managed to fix his hair without a mirror. I decided to suspend judgment and read further. Good decision. It turns out that what he actually meant was that you cannot know yourself by yourself.

Here’s the problem: we all have preconceived notions and biases that fight against reality. It actually is possible to look at yourself + + 3 2 in the mirror and see something different than what everyone else sees. Think, for example, of someone who has an eating disorde–the reflection staring back never looks thin enough. The example may sound drastic, but similar distortions of reality affect each of us in some aspect of our existence. I tend to think either too highly or too lowly of myself, depending on the day and the subject matter. Both of these mindsets are problematic because they cause me to act in ways that are out of line with reality. We need other people to know us so that we can in effect know ourselves. Seeing ourselves through another person’s eyes helps a lot, but that is still not enough. This other person has their own biases--various splinters in their eyes that create blind spots—but in the context of a community, these biases begin to balance out. It’s a filtering process, really.

If someone tells me I have a beautiful voice, like an opera singer, I might believe them. But if fifty additional people tell me that my voice is terrible, I might start to question the first person’s opinion. This person was either lying to me or has a curiously awful taste in music. A community’s opinion helps guard against the lies of subjectivity.

Relationships with other people are at the core of your relation-ship with God. At the start, your community gave you a language with which to read and hear the gospel, and God used the experiences and conversations in your life to bring you to a point where you decided to give up your old life of sin and death for new life in Christ. As you mature in faith, community remains at the core. In conversation with other Christ-followers, in the reading and discussion of the Bible together, and in corporate prayer, you gain a more robust experience of God than you would ever have on your own. Multiple viewpoints tend to capture reality better. Living a solitary life reflects a distorted reality. What’s more, it rarely produces the kind of tension that leads to life change. In a “just me and God” relationship, the effects of a depraved character are often difficult to grasp. By contrast, in honest and below-the-surface relationships with other people, the effects of sin are evident. Grace stops feeling so cheap as we witness the real consequences brought about by our selfish ambitions. To think other people are unnecessary in our relationship with God is short-sighted at best, and a different gospel at worst.

That’s not to say that God can’t reveal Himself to someone apart from using other people. We should not think, however, that because such a solo pursuit is possible, going it alone is either normative or prescriptive.

When God said that Adam was alone in the Garden (the first thing that was “not good”)1, I take that to mean that Adam was in some way lonely. That is, I do not think God was the only One who thought something was missing. Some of the guys in my Bible study do not buy this. They like to disagree with me, plus they do not think that Adam could have been lonely before the Fall. “He had perfect fellowship with God,” they say. I counter, “Loneliness is not a sin. Why can’t there be loneliness before the Fall?” You may have heard people say that we were created with a God-shaped void in our life. I read an argument recently that said we also were created with a human-shaped void in our life, a void that not even God Himself would fill. I haven’t checked this out with smart people yet, but I think that when God said, “Let us make man in our image, in our likeness,”2 He was at least partly, if not primarily, talking about community. Think about the Trinity for a moment … what do they do, how do they interact with each other? Just thinking of examples in Scripture, there is the baptism of Jesus when the Father says, “This is my Son whom I love, in Him I am well-pleased,” and the Spirit descends like a dove.3 At the transfiguration, the Father says about Jesus, “This 1 Genesis 2:18, NIV; 2 Genesis 1:26 (emphasis added, NIV); 3 Matthew 3:16-17, NIV + + 3 4 is my Son, whom I love. Listen to him!”4 Reciprocally, Jesus says He can only do what He sees His Father doing.5 Jesus also says that it will be better for His disciples when He leaves because the Spirit will come.6 The Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit are always deflecting glory to one another and serving each other. It is a community that creates together and works together and loves one another. Wouldn’t you like to have a group of friends like that, always trying to give each other credit and always serving the desires of one another in love? This is what God wants for us: “let us make man in our image, in our likeness,” that we might experience and live in community as God is in community.

Jesus restated this desire just before He went to the cross: “I will remain in the world no longer, but they are still in the world, and I am coming to you. Holy Father, protect them… so that they may be one as we are one. That all of them may be one, Father, just as you are in me and I am in you … that they may be one as we are one: I in them and you in me.”7 This sounds mushy to some people. What about evangelism and mission? Keep reading: “May they be brought to complete unity to let the world know that you sent me and have loved them even as you have loved me.”8 The design of creation is for a human version of Trinitarian community. Mission comes later, after the Fall, and is actually just an expression of what a God-centered, human-shaped-void-filling community would do.

It makes sense, then, that restored relationship with God is a communal experience and not an individual accomplishment. Think about how this plays out in the way we pursue knowledge, for instance. When I learn independently, all you see is the finished product. Perhaps I was just born with such wisdom; perhaps I have an inordinate IQ. Whatever the verdict, you will think better of me for it, and the appearance, however it appears, will 4 Mark 9:7; 5 See John 5:19; 6 See John 16:7; 7 John 17:11, 21-23 (emphasis added, NIV); 8 John 17:23 (emphasis added, NIV) not be accurate. When I learn in community, you can view me in process. A process whose humbling ingredients include me not knowing something, tactics of denial, being wrong, asking dumb questions, and being overly passionate trying to convince you that I’m right. It’s much better, in my opinion, to show up with the dispassionate product.

Our drive for independence glimpses the deformity of the Fall: God Himself living in community while we strive for self-sufficiency. I am not anti knowledge. I think learning is the essence of discipleship. I’m just saying that the kind of learning that leads to meaningful change requires community. It’s give and take, because while personal transformation requires community, a community is only as dynamic as the people in it. We are all in this together, each one of us needing what we must also give. A “me and God” approach is simply inadequate, inconceivable. When Debbie and I moved to Austin to do campus ministry at UT, I had no idea where to start. One of the guys on our team, Bob Thune, was supposed to show me the ropes. The first thing that Bob did was hand me a stack of thirty index cards with names and phone numbers on them. He said, “These are a bunch of freshmen fraternity guys who said they want to talk with someone about God.” I took the stack of cards like it was no big deal. It was a huge deal. The fraternity system was akin to the lion’s den in my mind, and I spent a month learning not to be scared of eighteen-year-olds. Sometime later Bob introduced me to a freshman he had met in another fraternity named Ben. He seemed nice, so I called him all the time when I was supposed to be doing something. The more I called the more he did not call me back. T wo years later he became the president and spiritual leader in his chapter. Now we labor together to give spiritual direction to a bunch of guys that are just like he was. + + 3 6 I have played several roles in Ben’s life: pest, mentor, friend, coworker, and recently the best man in his wedding. I like to think that I made Ben who he is. I like to think a lot of things that are not true. Just the opposite was illustrated powerfully at the rehearsal dinner. As I listened to Ben and Sarah’s family and friends tell stories about their childhood, I felt the smallness of my role. At one point someone in the family said her favorite saying is, “It takes a village to raise a child.” She really believed it and was pretty emotional about the sacrifice and effort of this particular village of people in their efforts to shape Ben and Sarah. Perhaps God feels the same way about us, that it takes a village. That is certainly what I have experienced over the past two years. I’d like to tell you about a few of the people in my village. I met Debbie in a rollerskating rink, just before spring break of our freshman year in college. I had plans to go see my cousin in Austin and then go to the beach. The first night in Austin, I couldn’t sleep. I kept thinking about Debbie, the way she had smiled at me when we said goodbye on campus. I had to see her. I didn’t know her phone number or how to get to her house, only that she lived in Dallas. I left my cousin’s apartment in the middle of the night and drove to my parent’s house in Ft. Worth. I tracked down Debbie’s number through my roommate, who knew Debbie’s roommate. I asked her out to a concert, which I thought was pretty cool. The problem was that I didn’t have any money. Being broke is what turned this romance into a comedy, or maybe a tragedy.

I figured if my parents wanted to come they would pay for every-thing. So that is how our first date went down, my parents and I picking up Debbie in my dad’s company car to go to a concert. On top of that, I invited Debbie’s roommate to come with us for some reason. You would have thought we were thirteen and going to the mall. The good part, I guess, is that I could only go up from there. So having failed miserably at being cool, I opted for being weird. I’m convinced that Debbie kept seeing me simply because she was intrigued. We married young (twenty-one) and have now been married nine years, some of them very difficult and some of them quite delightful. Debbie is straightforward and practical, and does not enjoy conversations that involve hypothetical scenarios. She grounds me in reality, but she also loves to dream of what we could be and do together. I long to be the person that she believes I am meant to be. I met Ethan, our son, in the delivery room. He has been with us four years now and he likes conversations that provide the answers to why everything is the way it is. Kids teach you in ways that no one else can. For me, Ethan clarifies what unconditional love must feel like.

I already mentioned Bob. Our time together at UT was cut short, against my will; something to do with God calling him to Omaha, as if a good God would ever call anyone to Omaha. Anyway, he moved and, without warning, I missed him. I started calling him on the phone, so much that my wife made fun of me. Bob and I developed a deep respect and affection for one another. Bob’s wife would tell me on occasion that he loves me. She said that he probably wouldn’t say it to me but that she wanted me to know. Two years ago I went through a rough time spiritually, which I have already alluded to. As I was coming out of it, I wrote some of my reflections down and sent them to Bob. He liked them, which is a pretty big deal, since he is not shy about telling me when he doesn’t like something. That gave me enough confidence to post the reflections on a blog and start writing about other things. It was all pretty selfish at first, but as others joined me (mainly Bob, David, and Brett), it became a community of learn-ing and restoration. We started writing stuff for each other to read, entertaining feedback, editing each other’s thoughts, and encouraging one another in what God was teaching all of us. The blog reflects our unique ways of seeing things. I am neurotically introspective. I like coffee and conversations that last longer than they should. Bob is a thinker and preacher, so he packs an intelligent punch. He likes books and conversations that last + + 3 8 exactly as long as they should. David is the elder among us, craft-ing the delicate art of cynical wisdom. He likes shepherding and conversations that let you say whatever you think you need to say. Brett is the youngest, but he is a freaking genius. And worse, he’s humble. He likes music and art and conversations that give him space for brevity. Others have occasionally chimed in on the blog with their thoughts, and still others read and discuss among their own circle of friends. Learning should have that effect, of inviting others to join the conversation, because again, each of us needs what only we can give.

My beginning with Brett is similar to Ben’s, except that Brett wasn’t in a fraternity and he called me back. We have spent count-less hours in Austin coffee shops talking about the life of faith. We try to articulate things we are thinking about and then exchange ideas and criticisms. I think of it as a test lab for ideas, each of us willing to have failed experiments. But we push each other to practice what is done in the lab in our actual lives. Much of what I have written and talked about in other communities has its origin in a conversation with Brett.

David was my boss when I started at UT. He has counseled and mentored me through what seems like every decision I have made over the last five years. He moved to Dallas last year, but we have not lost touch. His wife has not told me that he loves me, but I suspect that he does all the same.

The four of us–Bob, Brett, David, and I–are an unlikely group in an unlikely forum, a blog. But the blog’s only a manifestation, a metaphor really, for a life of give and take, encouragement, confrontation, and honesty. We couldn’t have known how our experiment in community was going to lead to such transformation. As I said, I did not expect to find that I need people. And I certainly did not expect that loving and being loved by them would be the primary way we experience God.
