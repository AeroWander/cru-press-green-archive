Intro to Christ-centered questions

By Rick Hove

758 words · 3 min read · Handout · For small-group leaders

In brief

An article by Rick Hove for small-group leaders on writing discussion questions that are both 'heart-targeting' and 'Christ-centered,' arguing that mere knowledge doesn't produce life change but wanting to change does. It contrasts generic Bible-study questions with examples of Christ-centered ones designed to expose the heart and point back to Christ.

Small groupsBible study & interpretation

Using Christ-Centered Questions That Target the Heart

I. Perspective

A. Why ask questions that target the heart?

  1. Life-change rarely occurs from simple knowledge. Think of all the things you know you should do. Does this ensure that you do them? Exercise? Control your eating? Thoughts? Study? Share your faith? Memorize the Bible?

Rather, life-change is more often like this: knowledge ⇒ “want to” ⇒ action. Someone can know, for example, that he should end a certain dating relationship but he will not do it until, at some level, he wants to. In other words, the heart is central to life-change.

We need to ask questions that target the heart – that both expose the ugly parts of our hearts but also point our hearts to Christ so that we will want to change in response to who He is. If our questions are simply “knowledge” or “how to” questions they will have limited effectiveness.

  1. One of the central motifs of the Scriptures is that God wants our hearts. Think white-washed tombs. Much of Christianity today fails to touch the heart. Furthermore, the root of sin lies at the heart level – cursing God, resisting God, ignoring God, making ourselves God, etc.

When questions target the heart they expose sin and point the way to the glorious Savior and the awesome gospel.

B. Why ask questions that are “Christ-centered”?

  1. From this point in salvation-history believers view the entire Bible through the lens of Christ. This isn’t to say that Christ is hiding in some obscure verse in Lamentations. Rather, it is saying that we read Lamentations, Psalms, Matthew or whatever from a post-Christ perspective, aware of who Christ is and what he has done.

Christ, then, should be at the center of the Christian life, for believers and unbelievers. It only makes sense that our “sub-text” should be “Isn’t Christ great?”

  1. One of the major themes of the NT is our resistance to Christ. We may, for example, judge others but at the root of this sin is some sort of resistance to Christ. Making questions “Christ-centered” gets to both the root of the problem and the solution to the problem.

II. How to write Christ-centered questions

A. Some examples of questions that are not specifically Christ-centered

Here are some questions that aren’t outright poor questions but they are neither Christ-centered nor target the heart:

  1. How can we trust God in the hard times of life?
  1. What are some ways we can honor God with our tongue?
  1. In what ways are we “aliens” on this earth?
  1. What difference does it make that Christ is God?

Note: all questions do not have to be “Christ-centered questions that target the heart.” But ideally, questions build to these types of questions.

B. Some examples of Christ-centered questions targeting the heart

  1. Think of some of the darkest thoughts that have ever crossed your mind – thoughts which deeply haunt you. Christ knows these thoughts. How does this make you feel?
  1. What in your personal agenda clashes with following Christ fully?
  1. What are you turning to for love in your life right now? If you turned to Christ for love, how would it affect your relationships with others?
  1. What are things apart from God on which you build your identity? If your identity was in Christ how would you be different?
  1. What are some reasonable responses –attitudes, emotions, and actions – to a Jesus who knows all?

6.What’s the most soul-gripping thing you’ve ever anticipated . . . some thing, some person, or some event that you LONGED for? Tell us about it. Do you LONG for the appearing of Christ with the same soul-gripping intensity that you long for other things? If not, why not?

  1. The greatest insult to Christ was the cross – the place of greatest shame. He was naked. Hung as a criminal when innocent. Suspended on the tree he made. Taunted by jeers to save himself (like he couldn’t). Isaiah puts it so powerfully:

“He was oppressed, and he was afflicted, yet he opened not his mouth; like a lamb that is led to slaughter, and like a sheep that is silent before its shearers is silent, so he opened not his mouth.”

Is 53:7

What is uniquely beautiful about Christ at this moment? Or, put another way, how is his beauty different at this moment than, say, when he raised Lazarus from the dead? What is beautiful about a God like this?
